1. The event

People start and stop filming whenever they like. Every phone clock runs a little fast or slow, some phones stand far away or in echoey corners, and nobody shares a clock. Scenefold never sees the facts on these cards; it only gets the clips.

2. Ingest: make every clip alike

Each file is read, given an ID from its fingerprint, kept untouched, and turned into a working copy: 720p, a steady 30 frames per second, upright, plus a mono 48 kHz sound file. Picture and sound start at the same instant, clip time 0, and the sound is gently stretched to follow the file's own timestamps, like the picture: some phones write more sound samples than their timestamps say (a Galaxy S II, 314 ppm). Problems become warnings, never crashes.

3. Match two clips by their sound

Slide clip B along clip A and score how well the sounds line up at every shift. The best shift is the lag. Whitening flattens the sound first so every pitch counts about equally, which turns the match into a sharp spike. Confidence is the best spike divided by the best one outside ±100 ms.

4. Clock drift: the lag slowly slides

If one phone's clock runs fast, its sound slowly slides against the other's, and one long match smears into a low, wide bump. So the overlap is cut into 10-second windows, each window's lag is measured, and a straight line through them gives the drift. Clip B is stretched to cancel it and matched again: the spike comes back sharp.

5. Solve all clips together

Every pair gets matched. Pairs that overlap too little, match too weakly, or match in only part of their overlap (the same song on another night) are set aside. The rest are solved together, weighted by confidence, and each pair is then checked against the answer worked out without it. The worst pair beyond 20 ms is dropped and the solve repeats. Clips that never overlap still get placed through the clips between them.

    6. Check accuracy with claps

    A clap is one instant that several phones catch. Put each phone's clap on the master timeline using that clip's offset and drift: if sync is right, they all land on the same spot. The error of a pair is how far apart its two clips put the same clap. One video frame is 33 ms.

    8. Watch them together

    Every placed clip plays at once on one master clock, which follows the frames of the clip being heard, so its sound is never sped up or slowed down. Every other video is checked on each frame it shows and steered: 0.3% faster or slower while on time, at least 1% to catch up, a jump when far off. Real players are slightly off in speed and sometimes stall; switch steering off and watch the pictures drift apart.

    Tiles flash when the frame they show catches a clap. In sync, they flash together.

    9. Sound takes time to arrive

    Light reaches every phone at once; sound crawls, about one metre every 2.9 milliseconds. Sync listens, so it lines up the moment each phone heard the event — and then a phone standing further back has its picture placed ahead of everyone else's, by exactly its extra distance, so its flash comes first. On two stadium concerts this reached 423 ms, twelve frames. So sync matches the placed clips a second time by their brightness, which never listens, and writes down how much later than the nearest phone each one heard the event. The viewer can then hold the pictures together instead of the sound.
