1. The event

People start and stop filming whenever they like. Every phone clock runs a little fast or slow, some phones stand far away or in echoey corners, and nobody shares a clock. Scenefold never sees the facts on these cards; it only gets the clips.

Real time (seconds). Dashed lines are claps that every nearby phone hears.

2. Ingest: make every clip alike

Each file is read, given an ID from its fingerprint, kept untouched, and turned into a working copy: 720p, a steady 30 frames per second, upright, plus a mono 48 kHz sound file. Picture and sound start at the same instant, clip time 0, and the sound is gently stretched to follow the file's own timestamps, like the picture: some phones write more sound samples than their timestamps say (a Galaxy S II, 314 ppm). Problems become warnings, never crashes.

3. Match two clips by their sound

Slide clip B along clip A and score how well the sounds line up at every shift. The best shift is the lag. Whitening flattens the sound first so every pitch counts about equally, which turns the match into a sharp spike. Confidence is the best spike divided by the best one outside ±100 ms.

4. Clock drift: the lag slowly slides

If one phone's clock runs fast, its sound slowly slides against the other's, and one long match smears into a low, wide bump. So the overlap is cut into 10-second windows, each window's lag is measured, and a straight line through them gives the drift. Clip B is stretched to cancel it and matched again: the spike comes back sharp.

5. Solve all clips together

Every pair gets matched. Pairs that overlap too little, match too weakly, or match in only part of their overlap (the same song on another night) are set aside. The rest are solved together, weighted by confidence, and each pair is then checked against the answer worked out without it. The worst pair beyond 20 ms is dropped and the solve repeats. Clips that never overlap still get placed through the clips between them.

    6. Check accuracy with claps

    A clap is one instant that several phones catch. Put each phone's clap on the master timeline using that clip's offset and drift: if sync is right, they all land on the same spot. The error of a pair is how far apart its two clips put the same clap. One video frame is 33 ms.

    8. Watch them together

    Every placed clip plays at once on one master clock, which follows the frames of the clip being heard, so its sound is never sped up or slowed down. Every other video is checked on each frame it shows and steered: 0.3% faster or slower while on time, at least 1% to catch up, a jump when far off. Real players are slightly off in speed and sometimes stall; switch steering off and watch the pictures drift apart.

    9. Sound takes time to arrive

    Light reaches every phone at once; sound crawls, about one metre every 2.9 milliseconds. Sync listens, so it lines up the moment each phone heard the event — and then a phone standing further back has its picture placed ahead of everyone else's, by exactly its extra distance, so its flash comes first. On two stadium concerts this reached 423 ms, twelve frames. So sync matches the placed clips a second time by their brightness, which never listens, and writes down how much later than the nearest phone each one heard the event. The viewer can then hold the pictures together instead of the sound.

    10. Cut the angles into one film

    Each second of each clip is scored on how good its picture is: sharp, steady, well exposed. Then the whole film is planned at once. A cut has to earn its keep, a shot has to last a few seconds, and cutting straight back to the angle you just left costs extra. Drag the dials and watch the film rebuild itself: cheap cuts give a restless film, dear ones a slow one.

    That is everything a camera can be wrong about and nothing about what it was pointed at, so a sharp shot of the floor would beat a shaky shot of the moment everyone came for. Tick knowing what happened and the cut also reads the events from step 12: how much happened in each second, weighted by how many phones caught it, and how much of that each angle has evidence for. It is added to the picture score, never replacing it, so an angle wins a moment by having both seen it and been worth looking at — and cutting across a moment costs more than cutting on the quiet in front of it.

    11. Watch each clip on its own

    A model on this computer looks at one clip at a time, in windows of ten seconds, four frames to a window, and says in one line what it can see. Its sense of time is only as fine as the window it was shown, so when comes from arithmetic instead: sound that suddenly grows, and pictures that suddenly change. Both are measured against the clip they come from and kept only where they rise four robust deviations above the usual run of it, so one firework does not raise the bar for everything else. Each window's line is then pulled onto the moment inside it that stands out most. No clip is ever shown another, which is what makes two angles two witnesses instead of one.

    12. Merge what every clip saw

    Every clip's moments are moved onto the shared clock, and the ones that land within 0.15 s of each other are taken to be one thing happening, caught by several phones. A clip only ever lends a group its strongest moment, so a phone that shakes twice in a tenth of a second does not become two witnesses. Two phones catching a moment together makes it far likelier that something happened; it still says nothing about what, which is a separate question and one only a reader can settle. Where a clip was filming with as good a picture and caught nothing, that is written down as a disagreement rather than smoothed away.

    t_master = offset_s + heard_late_s + t_local / (1 + drift_ppm / 1e6)

    13. Tell what happened, with citations

    The account is written from the store and nowhere else. The timeline is cut into as many slices as there are moments to tell and each slice offers its best-attested one, so the account covers the event rather than its noisiest minute. The writer gets that numbered list and nothing else, and ends each sentence with the number it came from. The marks stay where they were written, because "the screens at [3] and [4]" does not survive having them pulled out. Then the citations are checked in code, not by the model: the moment has to exist, and a clip that saw it has to have been filming then. A sentence that fails is dropped and the reason kept, so what was thrown away is as visible as what was left.

        14. Who is who across the angles

        Each clip is asked who it can see and what they are wearing: clothing, never faces and never names. Within a clip those sightings are joined window by window, so the red top in one window and the red top in the next are one person, while two people in the same window stay two people however alike they look. Then the clips are matched against each other on the shared clock. A description with no colour-and-garment pair in it ("a person", "dark jeans and a jacket") is dropped before any of that, because it picks out everybody. Nothing is forced: somebody only one phone filmed stays one person seen from one angle, and a match that only just clears the bar is reported as worth checking rather than as fact.

        alike = 0.75 × colour-and-garment pairs shared + 0.25 × bare colours shared, each against the smaller set, + 0.1 × time together
